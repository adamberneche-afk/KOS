## Prompt

Assignment: The Cavalier Shop Survey

You are a market researcher for the Cavalier Shop. Your job is to build a short survey that finds out how to reach our customers and what they want, without pushing them toward an answer.

Part 1: The Target Market. Who should take this survey: students, parents or staff? Pick one group and explain why they matter most to the store.

Part 2: Build the Survey. In Google Forms, build a survey with these three questions, set up exactly as shown:

- Question 1 (checkboxes, select up to two): How do you most frequently receive news and updates regarding Clover Hill High School events? Options: Canvas Announcements, CHHS Instagram, ParentSquare, Morning Announcements, Word of Mouth/Friends.
- Question 2 (linear scale, 1 = Not at all important, 5 = Extremely important): On a scale of 1 to 5, how important is it to you that the profits from your merchandise purchases directly fund CHHS student activities?
- Question 3 (short answer): What is one specific type of product or service you would like to see offered at The Cavalier Shop that is currently unavailable?

Turn off email collection in the form's settings. Paste your form's link into this document.

Part 3: Why These Questions Work. For each question, say whether it collects quantitative or qualitative data, and explain what the store will learn from it.

Part 4: Spot the Bias. Each question below is biased. For each one, explain what's wrong with it, then rewrite it so it's neutral.

- "How much do you love our amazing new t-shirts?"
- "Don't you agree the store should stay open after school?"
- "Would you rather buy a cheap hoodie online or a quality Cavalier hoodie that supports your school?"

Use these terms accurately: market research, target market, survey, quantitative data, qualitative data, bias, leading question.

## Background

A business that guesses what customers want wastes money on products nobody buys. Market research means collecting information from real customers before making decisions. A survey is one of the simplest ways to do it.

You can't market to everyone. A store that tries to appeal to everyone ends up appealing to no one. Freshmen who want spirit wear, parents with the credit cards and teachers buying a staff shirt all want different things and hear about events in different places. Picking a target market means deciding which group you're trying to reach.

Two kinds of data:

- Quantitative data is numbers you can count and compare: multiple choice, checkboxes, rating scales. "62% of students hear about events on Instagram" is quantitative.
- Qualitative data is ideas and opinions in people's own words: short-answer and paragraph questions. It's harder to count but can surprise you with ideas you never thought of.

Bias is the enemy of good research. A biased question pushes people toward an answer, so the results tell you what you wanted to hear instead of the truth. Watch for these:

- Leading words: "amazing," "great," "awful" tell people how to feel.
- Leading questions: "Don't you agree…?" makes it awkward to say no.
- Loaded choices: when one option is described as clearly better ("cheap" vs. "quality"), people pick the "right" one.
- Too-narrow options: a drop-down list can only collect the answers you already thought of. That's why Question 3 is open-ended.

Respect people's privacy. Turning off email collection lets people answer honestly without worrying about who will see their name.

## Key terms

- Market research: collecting information about customers to make better business decisions.
- Target market: the specific group of customers a business is trying to reach.
- Survey: a set of questions asked to many people to collect information.
- Quantitative data: information in numbers that can be counted and compared.
- Qualitative data: information in words, like opinions and ideas.
- Bias: anything in a question that pushes people toward a certain answer.
- Leading question: a question worded to suggest the "right" answer.

## Example

Fixing a biased question, step by step.

- Biased: "How great was the new snack selection at our last home game?"
- What's wrong: "great" tells people how to feel, and it assumes they liked the selection.
- Neutral: "How would you rate the snack selection at the last home game?" (scale of 1 = Very poor to 5 = Excellent)
- Why it's better: someone who disliked the snacks can say so just as easily as someone who liked them, so the store gets honest data.
