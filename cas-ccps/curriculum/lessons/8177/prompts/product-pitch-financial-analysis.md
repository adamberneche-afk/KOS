## Prompt

Assignment: The Buyer's Product Pitch

You are the merchandise buyer for the Cavalier Shop. Our products are made right here in the classroom, on the sublimation printer, heat press and large-format printer. Pitch one new product we should make and sell, and prove to the General Manager that it will make money.

Part 1: The Product. Name the product and describe it: what it is, what it looks like, and what makes it different from what the store already sells.

Part 2: The Why. Explain why CHHS students, families and fans will buy it. Who is it for, and when will they want it (a season, a big game, a spirit week)?

Part 3: The Materials. Name a real supplier for the blank item (with a link) and list everything else one finished item needs (ink, transfer paper, packaging). Give the cost of each, the minimum order and any shipping fees, and add them up to a cost per finished item. Then estimate how many the class can make in one class period.

Part 4: The Price. Give your retail price and explain the strategy behind it.

Part 5: The Math. Show every calculation: cost per finished item, profit per item, margin, total batch cost, break-even point, expected profit if the whole batch sells, and how long you expect it to take to sell out (turnover).

Use real numbers from a real supplier. Your product must be something our equipment can make, and it must follow the CHHS Student Handbook.

Use these terms accurately: SKU, supplier, cost, retail price, margin, break-even point, turnover.

## Background

A store has limited shelf space, so every product has to earn its spot. Think of shelf space like rent: if a product doesn't sell, it's taking up space a better product could use. A buyer's job is to find products customers want and that make money for the store. A product you like isn't automatically a good business decision. The numbers have to work.

The Cavalier Shop is also a manufacturer: we buy blank items and make the finished product ourselves. That changes the math. Our cost isn't a supplier's price for a finished shirt; it's the blank plus everything we use to decorate it. And our classroom equipment can only make so many items per class period, so production time matters too.

How a buyer decides:

- Know the customer. Who will buy it, and why? School pride, a big rivalry game, cold weather and gift-giving season all drive what sells.
- Find the materials. A supplier (or vendor) sells blank items (shirts, towels, tumblers) and supplies (ink, transfer paper) in bulk. Suppliers often have a minimum order. Sublimation works best on white or light-colored polyester, so check that the blank will work with our equipment.
- Add up the cost per finished item: the blank, plus the ink and paper, plus any packaging.
- Set the price. The retail price has to cover the cost and leave a profit, but it also has to be a price students will actually pay. Common strategies: ending a price in .99 so it feels lower ($9.99 instead of $10), pricing to match similar items, or bundling two items for a deal.
- Run the numbers before ordering. The store pays for materials up front, before a single item sells, so the buyer needs to know how many must sell just to get that money back.
- Check production time. If the heat press can finish about 25 items in a class period, an order of 200 takes 8 class periods, and the press can't be used for anything else during that time.

The formulas:

- Cost per finished item = Blank + ink and paper + packaging
- Profit per item = Retail price − Cost per finished item
- Margin = (Retail price − Cost per finished item) ÷ Retail price, written as a percent
- Total batch cost = (Cost per finished item × Number made) + shipping fees
- Break-even point = Total batch cost ÷ Profit per item. This is how many you must sell to get the store's money back.
- Expected profit = (Retail price × Number made) − Total batch cost
- Turnover: how fast the order sells out, for example "in about five weeks."

## Key terms

- SKU (Stock Keeping Unit): the code a store uses to track one specific product, like one color and size of one shirt.
- Buyer: the person who chooses which products a store sells and orders them.
- Supplier (vendor): a company that sells blank items and supplies in bulk.
- Blank: an undecorated item (like a plain white polyester shirt) that we print our design onto.
- Sublimation: a printing method that uses heat to turn special ink into a gas that bonds with polyester, so the design becomes part of the fabric.
- Cost: what it costs the store to make one finished item.
- Retail price: what the customer pays the store.
- Margin: the share of the retail price the store keeps as profit, as a percent.
- Break-even point: the number of items the store must sell to earn back what it spent on the order.
- Turnover: how quickly a product sells and has to be restocked.
- ROI (return on investment): the profit compared with what was spent, as a percent.

## Example

A rally towel pitch, with the math (illustrative numbers; yours must come from a real supplier). Blank white polyester towels cost $1.60 each, with a 100-towel minimum and $20 shipping. Ink and transfer paper add about $0.40 per towel.

- Cost per finished towel: $1.60 + $0.40 = $2.00
- Total batch cost: ($2.00 × 100) + $20 = $220
- Retail price: $8.00, less than a ticket, so it's an easy add-on at a game
- Profit per towel: $8.00 − $2.00 = $6.00
- Margin: $6.00 ÷ $8.00 = 75%
- Break-even: $220 ÷ $6.00 ≈ 36.7, so 37 towels. After the 37th towel sells, the rest is profit.
- Expected profit if all 100 sell: ($8.00 × 100) − $220 = $580
- ROI: $580 ÷ $220 ≈ 264%
- Production: at about 25 towels per class period, the batch takes 4 class periods to make.
- Turnover: at about 20 towels a week during football season, the batch sells out in about 5 weeks.
