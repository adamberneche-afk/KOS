## Prompt

Assignment: The Cavalier Shop Planogram

Design a better layout for the Cavalier Shop. Treat the store like a small venue: your map should help customers find what they want, move through without crowding, and stay safe.

Part 1: Your Map. Add a top-down map of the store to this document (Insert, then Drawing, or a photo of a neat hand-drawn sketch). Show the entrance, the exits, the register and every shelf, rack and table.

Part 2: Traffic Flow. Draw arrows showing the path most customers will take. In two or three sentences, explain where a bottleneck could form during a lunch rush and how your layout prevents it.

Part 3: Zones. Label at least three merchandise zones. Explain where your hot zone is, what you put there and why. Then explain where impulse buys go and why.

Part 4: Safety and Staff. Mark the exits and the 36-inch clear walkways. Mark where each staff member stands during a shift, and explain why each spot helps customers or prevents loss.

Use these terms accurately: planogram, traffic flow, hot zone, bottleneck, impulse buy, atmospherics.

## Background

Stores and stadiums are planned on purpose. Before a stadium opens, managers plan where fans will walk, where lines will form and where the concession stands go, so the crowd keeps moving and spends money. A store works the same way on a smaller scale. The plan for where every shelf and product goes is called a planogram.

What a good layout does:

- Guides traffic. Customers follow a path through the store. A good layout leads them past more products without making them feel lost or crowded. Many stores are designed on the idea that most shoppers turn right after they walk in.
- Uses the hot zone. The spot customers see first, usually just inside the entrance and facing them, gets the most attention. Stores put new arrivals and best sellers there.
- Places impulse buys at checkout. Small, low-priced items near the register (pins, lanyards, snacks) get added at the last second while customers wait.
- Groups items logically. Merchandise zones keep similar products together, like a cold-weather zone with hoodies and beanies, so customers find things fast.
- Sets the mood. Atmospherics are the lighting, music, colors and signs that make a store feel like part of the Cavalier brand.

Safety comes first:

- Keep walkways at least 36 inches wide and clear of boxes. This is required for wheelchair access (ADA) and for getting out fast in an emergency.
- Never block an exit or a door with merchandise or displays.

Staff placement matters too. Where employees stand decides how fast customers get help and how easy it is to notice theft. Someone near the entrance can greet customers, and someone at the register can watch the checkout line.

## Key terms

- Planogram: a map or diagram showing where every shelf, fixture and product goes in a store.
- Traffic flow: the path customers take as they move through a store or venue.
- Hot zone: the area customers notice first and visit most, used for the products the store most wants to sell.
- Bottleneck: a narrow spot where people get stuck and lines back up.
- Impulse buy: a small purchase a customer decides to make on the spot, often at the register.
- Merchandise zone: a section of the store where similar products are grouped together.
- Atmospherics: the lighting, music, colors and decor that create the feel of a store.
- Egress: the way out of a building, especially in an emergency.

## Example

One part of a sample layout. "Customers enter on the left side of the front wall. Straight ahead, a table of new spirit wear sits in the hot zone, so it's the first thing they see. The cold-weather zone (hoodies and beanies) is on the back wall, which pulls customers through the whole store. The register is by the exit, with pins and lanyards on the counter as impulse buys. The walkway from the door to the register stays 40 inches wide so the lunch line doesn't block it. One associate stands by the entrance to greet customers, and the Shift Manager works the register where they can see the whole floor."
