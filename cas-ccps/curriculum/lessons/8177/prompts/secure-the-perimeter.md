## Prompt

Assignment: The Emergency Action Plan

It's a sold-out rivalry football game at Clover Hill. Then the crisis hits: lightning is spotted nearby, the game is delayed, and 3,000 fans are sitting on metal bleachers. You're the director of security. Write the emergency action plan.

The simplified stadium layout:

- Home bleachers (east side): 2,000 fans, metal, with two stairways down to the track
- Visitor bleachers (west side): 1,000 fans, metal, with one stairway
- Main gate (south end): the only entrance, about 20 feet wide, leading to the parking lots
- Emergency gate (north end): a 10-foot gate, normally locked, leading toward the school building
- School gym: about 300 yards from the north gate; can shelter about 1,500 people
- Staff: 6 police officers, 12 guest services staff (in yellow shirts), 2 athletic trainers, 1 PA announcer

Part 1: The Plan. Where should fans go, and why? Use what you know about lightning safety.

Part 2: The Map. Draw a simple map of the stadium (by hand or in Google Drawings) and add it to this document. Use arrows to show the routes each section of fans will take. Show how you'll keep the main gate from becoming a bottleneck.

Part 3: The Staff. Explain where you'll send the police officers, the guest services staff and the athletic trainers, and what each group's job is during the evacuation.

Part 4: The PA Announcement. Write the exact three-sentence announcement the PA announcer will read. It should keep people calm and tell them exactly where to go.

Part 5: Connect It. Name one drill we practice at school (fire, tornado or lockdown) and explain how it's an emergency action plan for the school as a venue.

Use these terms accurately: emergency action plan, crowd control, ingress, egress, bottleneck, shelter in place, evacuation.

## Background

The best marketing and the most profitable pricing mean nothing if people aren't safe. A venue's first job is to protect people, then property, then its reputation. A single emergency handled badly can injure people and ruin a venue's name for years. One handled well builds trust.

Crowd control:

- Ingress is people coming in; egress is people going out.
- A bottleneck forms when too many people try to fit through too small a space, like one gate for 3,000 fans. In a panic, bottlenecks can cause dangerous crowd surges.
- Good plans spread people across every available exit and give each section a clear route.

People and technology both keep fans safe:

- Technology: clear-bag policies, metal detectors, security cameras (CCTV), ticket scanners and two-way radios.
- Police officers handle enforcement: threats, fights, traffic and anything dangerous.
- Guest services staff (often in bright shirts so they're easy to find) handle people: directions, calm voices, helping someone who's lost or hurt. Their job is to de-escalate and guide.
- Athletic trainers and medical staff handle injuries and health emergencies.

Emergency action plans (EAPs) are written before anything goes wrong, so everyone knows their job in a crisis. Two main responses:

- Evacuation: getting everyone out of an area to a safer place.
- Shelter in place: keeping people where they are, or moving them to a safe space nearby, when going outside is more dangerous.

Lightning safety: there is no safe place outdoors during a thunderstorm. Metal bleachers and open fields are especially dangerous. People should move into a sturdy building or a hard-topped car. A common rule for school sports is to wait until 30 minutes have passed since the last lightning or thunder before going back outside.

A good PA announcement is short, calm and specific: what's happening, where to go, how to get there. Avoid words that cause panic.

## Key terms

- Emergency action plan (EAP): a written plan for what everyone does in an emergency.
- Crowd control: managing how people move through a venue to keep them safe.
- Ingress: entering a venue.
- Egress: exiting a venue.
- Bottleneck: a narrow point where people back up.
- Evacuation: moving everyone out of a dangerous area.
- Shelter in place: staying in, or moving to, a nearby safe space instead of leaving.

## Example

A sample PA announcement for a different emergency: a power outage at an indoor concert.

"Ladies and gentlemen, we've lost power in the building and the show is paused while our crew works on it. Please stay in your seats, and if you need to move, follow the lighted signs and our staff in yellow shirts. We'll update you in five minutes."

## Warm-up

Think about a fire drill or a tornado drill at school. What makes it go smoothly? What could go wrong if it were a real emergency with 3,000 people?
