## Prompt

Assignment: Survey Fieldwork

The class needs 323 real survey responses from CHHS students to trust the results. Your share is 15 unique responses. Your teacher will give you the survey link. This document is where you plan your fieldwork and keep track of it.

Part 1: Why 323? In your own words, explain why the store needs 323 responses instead of asking 5 or 10 friends. What could go wrong if it made decisions from too few answers?

Part 2: Your Pitch. Write the 10-second pitch you will use to ask people to take the survey. It should say who you are, what the survey is for, and how quick it is.

Part 3: Your Plan. When and where will you collect responses (lunch, homeroom, before or after school)? Who will you ask? Make sure your plan reaches more than just your closest friends.

Part 4: Your Log. Keep a running count as you go: the date, where you were, and how many people took the survey. Do not write anyone's name.

Part 5: Reflect. When you're done, answer: What worked best? What would you do differently? Why does it matter that every response is real?

Rules: each person takes the survey only once; you may count yourself as one of your 15; collect only during approved times, never during another teacher's class. Fake or duplicate responses earn a zero.

Use these terms accurately: population, sample, sample size, statistical significance, margin of error.

## Background

A business that orders 200 hoodies because five of its friends said they'd buy one is taking a big risk. Five people aren't enough to know what the whole school wants. To trust survey results, you need enough answers from enough different people.

Population and sample:

- The population is everyone you want to learn about: here, all CHHS students.
- A sample is the smaller group you actually survey.
- If the sample is big enough, and picked from across the whole population, its answers will be close to what the whole school would say.

Why 323? Statisticians have formulas for how big a sample needs to be. For a school of about 2,000 students, about 323 responses lets you be 95% confident that your results are within 5 percentage points of what the whole school thinks. That 5 points is the margin of error. A smaller sample means a bigger margin of error, and less trust in the results.

What statistical significance means here: results from a big enough sample are reliable enough to act on. When 60% of 323 students say they want a product, the store can order it with confidence. When 3 of 5 friends say so, it can't.

Getting a good sample:

- Spread out. If you only ask your friends, or only athletes, or only seniors, your sample won't look like the whole school.
- Keep it honest. One response per person, no made-up answers. One fake response doesn't just hurt your grade. It makes the whole class's data wrong.
- Ask politely and keep it short. People say yes to a quick, friendly ask from someone who respects their time.

## Key terms

- Population: the whole group you want to learn about.
- Sample: the smaller group you actually survey.
- Sample size: the number of people in the sample.
- Statistical significance: when results come from a large enough sample to be trusted, not just chance.
- Margin of error: how far the survey results could be from the true answer for the whole population.
- Unique response: one survey from one person, counted only once.

## Example

A sample pitch and log entry.

- Pitch: "Hi, I'm in the Sports Management class. We're running a 1-minute survey to help the Cavalier Shop decide what to sell. Would you scan this and take it?"
- Log entry: "Tuesday, lunch block B, cafeteria: 4 responses (asked 6 people, 2 said no)."
