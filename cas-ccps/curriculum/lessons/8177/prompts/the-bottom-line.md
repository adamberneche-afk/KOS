## Prompt

Assignment: The $10,000 Tip-Off Budget

You're the chief financial officer (CFO) of a weekend charity basketball tournament in the Clover Hill gym. You have $10,000 in seed money to cover costs up front, and your goal is to raise money for charity: the event must earn a profit margin of at least 15%.

The vendor price list:

- Referees: $50 an hour each; 2 referees for 12 hours of games
- Custodian: $30 an hour; 14 hours (including setup and cleanup)
- Security officer: $40 an hour; 12 hours
- Athletic trainer: $35 an hour; 12 hours
- Gym facility fee: $300 a day for 2 days
- Event insurance: $200
- Trophies: $150
- Marketing (flyers and social media ads): $150
- Player t-shirts: $8 each (16 teams of 6 players)
- Concession items: cost $1.25 each to stock; you choose the selling price

What to expect:

- 16 teams will register at a fee of $150 or less. At a higher fee, expect fewer teams.
- About 400 spectator tickets will sell at $5. At a higher price, expect fewer.
- Spectators buy about 1.25 concession items each.

Part 1: Projected Revenue. Choose your team registration fee, ticket price and concession price. Estimate how many of each you'll sell, and calculate the revenue from each source and in total.

Part 2: Projected Expenses. List every expense from the price list as its own line, and say whether each is a fixed cost or a variable cost. Calculate the total.

Part 3: The Bottom Line. Calculate net profit (revenue − expenses) and your profit margin (net profit ÷ revenue × 100). Did you reach 15%? If not, change something and try again, and show both versions. Build your budget in Google Sheets with formulas, and add the link to this document.

Part 4: The CFO Memo. Write a short memo to the board of directors that names the two biggest financial risks in your budget and what you'd do about each (for example, "If we sell fewer than 300 tickets, we lose money").

Your budget is confidential: share the sheet only with your teacher.

Use these terms accurately: budget, revenue, expenses, fixed cost, variable cost, net profit, profit margin.

## Background

An event can have great marketing, a perfect venue and huge demand, and still fail if it spends more than it brings in. In 2017, the Fyre Festival sold expensive luxury packages with a famous influencer marketing campaign, then ran out of money for tents, food and performers. The festival collapsed, and its founder went to prison for fraud. The marketing was an A+. The budget was an F.

How an event income statement (profit and loss, or P&L) is organized:

- Top line, revenue: all the money coming in (registration fees, tickets, concessions, merchandise, sponsors).
- Middle, expenses: all the money going out (venue, staff, insurance, supplies, marketing).
- Bottom line, net profit or loss: revenue minus expenses. That's where the phrase "the bottom line" comes from.

"In the black" means a profit. "In the red" means a loss.

Two kinds of costs:

- Fixed costs stay the same no matter how many people come: the gym fee, insurance, trophies.
- Variable costs go up or down with the crowd or the number of teams: concession stock, t-shirts.

Profit margin shows how much of each dollar of revenue you keep: net profit ÷ revenue × 100. A 15% margin means you keep 15 cents of every dollar that comes in.

Budget risks to watch: if your ticket estimate is too optimistic, revenue falls but most costs don't. Ordering concession stock you don't sell is money lost. Raising prices can bring in more per person but fewer people.

Keep financial information secure: in the real world, a P&L is confidential. If a rival promoter saw it, they'd know exactly what you pay referees and security and could outbid you. Share budget files only with the people who need them.

## Key terms

- Budget: a plan for how much money will come in and go out.
- Revenue: all the money an event brings in.
- Expenses: all the money an event spends.
- Fixed cost: a cost that stays the same no matter how many people attend.
- Variable cost: a cost that changes with the number of people or items.
- Net profit: revenue minus expenses.
- Profit margin: net profit as a percentage of revenue.
- Income statement (P&L): a financial report showing revenue, expenses and profit or loss.

## Example

A tiny P&L for a different event: a one-day student volleyball tournament.

- Revenue: 8 teams × $100 = $800; 150 tickets × $4 = $600. Total revenue: $1,400.
- Expenses: gym fee $300 (fixed), referees $400 (fixed), trophies $100 (fixed), team t-shirts 48 × $8 = $384 (variable). Total expenses: $1,184.
- Net profit: $1,400 − $1,184 = $216.
- Profit margin: $216 ÷ $1,400 × 100 ≈ 15.4%, so it just clears a 15% goal.
