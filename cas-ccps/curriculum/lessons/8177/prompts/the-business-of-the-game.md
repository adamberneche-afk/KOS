## Prompt

Assignment: The Corporate Scouting Report

Pick one professional sports team or major entertainment company (not the Green Bay Packers, which is the example). Forget wins and losses: study it as a business, and write a scouting report on it.

Part 1: The Company. Name the team or company and give its current value, with the source (Forbes publishes team values every year).

Part 2: The Owner. Who owns it, and what type of business ownership is it? Explain how you know.

Part 3: The Money. Name its top three sources of revenue. For each one, explain in a sentence how it brings in money.

Part 4: The Community. Describe one specific way this business affects its home city, good or bad (jobs, charity, a new stadium paid for with tax money, traffic on game days).

Part 5: The Competition. Name its biggest indirect competitor: something else its fans could spend a Friday night's money on. Explain why it competes.

List your sources at the end (website name and link). Use these terms accurately: revenue, profit, sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, direct competition, indirect competition.

## Background

Fans watch the scoreboard. Owners watch the balance sheet. A pro team is a business, and like any business it has to bring in more money than it spends.

Where the money comes from:

- Media rights: TV networks and streaming services pay leagues huge amounts to show the games. In the NFL, this national TV money is the biggest source of revenue, much bigger than ticket sales.
- Ticket sales (gate receipts): regular seats, season tickets, and premium seats and suites.
- Sponsorships and naming rights: companies pay to put their names on the stadium, the jerseys and the ads around the field.
- Merchandise and concessions: jerseys, hats, food and drinks.

Where the money goes: player salaries are usually a team's biggest expense, followed by coaches, staff, travel and running the stadium. Profit is what's left after paying all of it.

Types of business ownership:

- Sole proprietorship: one person owns the business and keeps the profits, but is also personally responsible for its debts.
- Partnership: two or more people own the business together and share the profits and the responsibility.
- Corporation: the business is its own legal "person," owned by shareholders. Owners can't lose more than what they put in.

In the real world, a team "owned by one family" is usually set up as a company the family controls, not a true sole proprietorship. The Dallas Cowboys have been owned by Jerry Jones and his family since 1989. The Green Bay Packers are the opposite: a nonprofit corporation owned by hundreds of thousands of fans who hold shares. Their shares pay no profit and can't be sold for a gain, but no single owner can ever move the team out of Green Bay.

Teams compete with more than other teams. Direct competition is another business selling the same thing (another team in town). Indirect competition is anything else people could spend the same money and time on: a concert, a movie, a streaming subscription, a night out with friends.

## Key terms

- Revenue: all the money a business brings in before paying any costs.
- Expenses: the money a business spends to operate.
- Profit: revenue minus expenses; what the business keeps.
- Media rights: the fees TV networks and streaming services pay to show games.
- Valuation: what a business is estimated to be worth if it were sold.
- Sole proprietorship: a business owned by one person.
- Partnership: a business owned by two or more people who share profits and responsibility.
- Corporation: a business that is its own legal entity, owned by shareholders.
- Direct competition: businesses selling the same kind of product to the same customers.
- Indirect competition: businesses selling something different that competes for the same customer's money or time.

## Example

A short scouting report on the Green Bay Packers (use a different team for yours).

- The Owner: the Packers are a nonprofit corporation owned by shareholders, most of them fans. No one person owns them, and their shares pay no dividends.
- The Money: the NFL's national TV deals, which every team shares; ticket sales at Lambeau Field, where season tickets have a long waiting list; and merchandise sold to fans around the country.
- The Community: Green Bay is the smallest city in the NFL, and the team is a major draw for visitors who spend money at local hotels and restaurants on game weekends.
- The Competition: on a fall Sunday, the indirect competition is staying home and watching the game on TV, which costs much less than a ticket.
