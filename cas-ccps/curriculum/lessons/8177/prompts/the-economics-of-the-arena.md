## Prompt

Assignment: The Pricing Strategy Brief

You are the Director of Ticketing for the River City Blades, a minor-league hockey team. The arena holds 5,000 fans. For each game below, the sales team has estimated how many tickets will sell at each price.

Opening Night (Saturday, first game of the season):

- $20: 5,000 tickets
- $30: 5,000 tickets
- $40: 4,600 tickets
- $50: 3,800 tickets
- $60: 3,000 tickets

Snowy Tuesday (weeknight, snow in the forecast):

- $10: 2,800 tickets
- $15: 2,400 tickets
- $20: 1,900 tickets
- $25: 1,400 tickets
- $30: 1,000 tickets

Playoff Game (championship round):

- $40: 5,000 tickets
- $60: 5,000 tickets
- $80: 4,800 tickets
- $100: 4,200 tickets
- $120: 3,300 tickets

Part 1: The Math. For each game, calculate the total revenue at every price (price × tickets sold). Show each calculation.

Part 2: Your Price. For each game, choose the ticket price you would charge. Explain why, using your math. If you chose a price that doesn't bring in the most ticket revenue, explain what you gain instead (a full arena, more concession sales, happier fans).

Part 3: Supply and Demand. Explain, in your own words, why the playoff game can charge so much more than the Tuesday game, even though it's the same team in the same arena.

Part 4: Is It Fair? Write one paragraph answering this question: is it fair for a team to charge one fan $100 for a seat on Saturday and another fan $20 for the same seat on Tuesday? Take a side and back it up.

Use these terms accurately: supply, demand, scarcity, revenue, fixed pricing, dynamic pricing.

## Background

Why does a ticket to a Tuesday night baseball game cost $15, while the same seat for a Saturday night game against the biggest rival costs $85? Same team, same stadium, same seat. The answer is supply and demand.

Supply and demand:

- Supply is how much of something is available. An arena has a fixed number of seats, so the supply of tickets for one game can't grow.
- Demand is how many people want it, and how much they're willing to pay.
- When demand is high and supply is fixed, prices go up. When demand is low, prices have to come down to fill seats.
- Scarcity is when there isn't enough of something for everyone who wants it. A stadium that holds 65,000 people with 2 million fans who want in is the definition of scarcity. When tickets are scarce, resellers can charge far more than face value.

Fixed pricing and dynamic pricing:

- Fixed pricing: a seat costs the same for every game all season (all lower-level seats are $50).
- Dynamic pricing: prices change based on demand: the opponent, the day of the week, the weather, whether a star player is playing, how many tickets are left. Teams and Ticketmaster use computer programs that update prices constantly, like airline tickets.

The revenue math:

- Total revenue = price × number of tickets sold.
- A higher price doesn't always mean more money. Raise the price too high and so many fans stay home that total revenue drops.
- The best price is often somewhere in the middle: the "sweet spot" where price × tickets is highest.
- Empty seats cost money too. A fan who doesn't come doesn't buy a hot dog, a soda or a jersey, so some teams price a little lower to fill the arena.

The fairness question: dynamic pricing helps teams earn more and can make cheap seats available on slow nights. But many fans feel it's unfair when prices jump the moment a big game or concert goes on sale, and it can price loyal fans out of the games they care about most.

## Key terms

- Supply: how much of a product is available to sell.
- Demand: how much of a product people want and are willing to pay for.
- Scarcity: when there isn't enough of something for everyone who wants it.
- Revenue: all the money brought in from sales (price × quantity).
- Fixed pricing: charging the same price no matter the demand.
- Dynamic pricing: changing prices up or down based on demand.
- Secondary market: tickets resold by fans or resellers after the original sale, often at a different price.

## Example

A different event, to show the method. A concert hall holds 2,000 people.

- $25 × 2,000 tickets = $50,000
- $35 × 1,800 tickets = $63,000
- $45 × 1,500 tickets = $67,500 (the highest revenue)
- $55 × 1,100 tickets = $60,500

The $45 price brings in the most ticket money, but 500 seats would be empty. If each concert-goer spends about $10 on food and merchandise, the 300 extra fans at $35 would add $3,000 in sales. Ticket revenue at $35 is $4,500 lower, so $45 still comes out ahead by $1,500. That's the kind of tradeoff a ticketing director has to weigh.
