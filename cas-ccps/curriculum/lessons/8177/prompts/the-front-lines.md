## Prompt

Assignment: The Front Lines

You'll role-play two jobs from the front lines of a venue with a partner: selling a premium package and calming an upset fan. Use this document to prepare your scripts, then reflect after you perform.

Scenario A, the sale: you're an account executive for a minor-league baseball team. A local car dealership owner is thinking about buying a 12-seat suite for the season ($15,000). They worry it costs too much and aren't sure how they'd use it.

Scenario B, the complaint: you work at the box office. A fan paid $150 for a seat and just found out it's directly behind a concrete pillar. They're furious and want a refund.

Part 1: The Sales Script. Write what you'll say for Scenario A at each of the six steps of the selling process. For the "determine needs" step, write at least two open-ended questions. For "present the product," turn three features of the suite into benefits for this owner.

Part 2: The Service Script. Write what you'll say for Scenario B at each step of the T.H.A.N.K.S. method. Include the solution you'll offer and why it's fair to both the fan and the team.

Part 3: Reflect. After you perform, answer: which step was hardest, what did your partner say that surprised you, and how did you adjust?

Use these terms accurately: selling process, open-ended question, feature, benefit, objection, suggestion selling, service recovery.

## Background

In sports and entertainment, the product is an experience, and the people at the front lines (ticket reps, box office staff, ushers) shape that experience more than anyone. How a problem is handled often matters more to a fan's loyalty than the problem itself. Keeping a customer is also much cheaper than finding a new one.

Two kinds of selling: B2C (business-to-consumer) is selling to individual fans. B2B (business-to-business) is selling to companies, like a suite for a business to entertain clients.

The six-step selling process:

- Approach: a warm, professional introduction that builds trust.
- Determine needs: ask open-ended questions (they can't be answered yes or no) to learn what the customer wants. "Who do you usually take to games?" works better than "Do you like baseball?"
- Present the product: show how the product meets their needs. Turn features into benefits.
- Overcome objections: when they push back ("It's too expensive"), don't argue. Find out what's behind it and answer with value.
- Close: ask for the sale. "Would you like to reserve the suite for the season?"
- Suggestion selling: offer something that adds value, like VIP parking or a catering package.

Features vs. benefits: a feature is a fact about the product ("The suite has 12 seats"). A benefit is what that fact does for the customer ("You can bring your top 10 clients and two of your best salespeople to every game"). People buy benefits.

Service recovery is fixing a bad experience so well that the customer leaves happier than before. The T.H.A.N.K.S. method:

- Thank the customer for bringing the problem to you.
- Hear them out: listen without interrupting.
- Apologize for the inconvenience, sincerely.
- Need more information: ask questions to understand exactly what happened.
- Know a solution: know what you're allowed to offer (a seat change, a refund, a future ticket).
- Solve the problem, then make sure they're satisfied.

Stay calm. When a fan is angry, they're upset about the situation, not you. Lower your voice, use their name, and focus on fixing it.

## Key terms

- Selling process: the six steps from first contact to closing the sale.
- Open-ended question: a question that can't be answered with yes or no.
- Feature: a fact about a product.
- Benefit: what a feature does for the customer.
- Objection: a concern or reason a customer gives for not buying.
- Suggestion selling: offering an additional product that adds value to the purchase.
- Service recovery: turning a customer's bad experience into a good one.
- Customer retention: keeping existing customers coming back.

## Example

Turning features into benefits, for a different product: a family 4-pack.

- Feature: four tickets, four hot dogs and four drinks for $60.
- Benefit: "You'll know exactly what the night costs before you leave home, and the kids won't be asking for snacks every inning."
- Objection: "We can just watch at home."
- Response: "Totally fair. A lot of families tell us the same thing. What they like about coming is the fireworks after the game and the kids running the bases, something you can't do from the couch."
