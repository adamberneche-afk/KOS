## Prompt

Assignment: The Millionaire's Strike

The league's contract with its players has expired, and the season is about to be canceled. Your class will split into the Owners and the Players Association and negotiate a new contract, called a collective bargaining agreement (CBA), on four issues:

- Revenue sharing: owners want 55% of TV money to the owners and 45% to the players. Players want 50/50.
- Season length: owners want 2 more games. Players refuse, citing injury risk.
- Minimum salary: players want rookies to earn at least $1,000,000. Owners want $750,000.
- Overseas play: owners want to ban players from playing in other countries' leagues in the off-season, to protect against injury. Players want the right to play anywhere.

If the two sides can't agree before the end of class, the season is canceled.

Part 1: Your Side. Say which side you're on. For each of the four issues, write what your side wants and one reason it's fair.

Part 2: The Deal. After the negotiation, record the final agreement on each issue, and explain what each side gave up to get there.

Part 3: The Collateral Damage Report. If a Richmond team (like the Flying Squirrels or the Kickers) stopped playing for a season, who would get hurt? Name three specific local businesses or hourly jobs, and for each one explain exactly how its income depends on game days.

Part 4: Who Has the Leverage? Explain why owners can't just fire striking players and hire replacements, and why players who can play overseas have extra bargaining power.

Use these terms accurately: union, collective bargaining agreement, strike, lockout, leverage, specialization, unemployment.

## Background

When pro athletes go on strike, it can look like millionaires arguing with billionaires. But a work stoppage hurts a lot of people who aren't rich at all, and it shows how labor and business work in every industry.

Athletes have unions too. Every major U.S. pro league has a players' union, like the NFL Players Association. A union is a group of workers who bargain with their employer together instead of one at a time. Athletes negotiate minimum salaries, health care and safety rules the same way teachers, plumbers and auto workers do.

The contract and the stoppage:

- Collective bargaining agreement (CBA): the contract between the owners and the players' union, usually for several years at a time.
- Strike: the players refuse to play until they get a better deal.
- Lockout: the owners refuse to let the players play until the players accept their terms.

Leverage is the power one side has in a negotiation. Players have leverage because of specialization. An elite quarterback or point guard has skills almost no one else in the world has. If owners replaced their stars with amateurs, the quality would drop and fans would stop paying. Players also have options: during the 2011 NBA lockout, some players signed with teams in Europe and Asia. Owners can't move their stadiums overseas.

The ripple effect: during a strike, the players and owners can usually afford to wait. The people who lose their paychecks are the ushers, ticket takers, concession workers, parking attendants and security staff, plus the restaurants, bars and hotels near the stadium that count on game-day crowds. When many people lose income at once, they spend less, and that spreads to other businesses. That's how a sports dispute turns into local unemployment and a slowdown for a whole city.

How to negotiate professionally: listen to the other side, look for trades ("we'll accept 52/48 if you drop the extra games"), and never insult the other side. In a real negotiation, a deal both sides can live with is better than no season at all.

## Key terms

- Union (organized labor): a group of workers who negotiate with their employer together.
- Collective bargaining agreement (CBA): the contract between a union and an employer.
- Strike: when workers refuse to work to pressure the employer.
- Lockout: when an employer refuses to let workers work to pressure the union.
- Leverage: the power one side has to get what it wants in a negotiation.
- Specialization: when workers have rare, specific skills that are hard to replace.
- Unemployment: when people who want to work can't find a job or lose their income.
- Productivity: how much value a worker produces compared with what they cost.

## Example

One entry from a sample Collateral Damage Report.

"A concession worker at the ballpark: many concession workers are paid by the hour and work only on game nights. With about 70 home games in a season, a canceled season would wipe out that income completely. A worker who depends on that money to pay for a car or help with rent would have to find another job fast, and if many workers are looking at once, there may not be enough jobs to go around."
