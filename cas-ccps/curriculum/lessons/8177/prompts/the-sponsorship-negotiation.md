## Prompt

Assignment: The Sponsorship Negotiation

You're the director of corporate partnerships for a CHHS team or event. Your goal: sell a $500 banner sponsorship to a local business. You'll build a list of prospects, prepare for the hard questions, and then negotiate with a partner playing a skeptical business owner.

Part 1: The CRM. In Google Sheets, build a simple customer relationship tracker with these columns: business name, contact person and title, contact information, why they're a good fit, and status (researching, ready to pitch, pitched, closed, declined). Add five real local businesses. At least two must be owned by women or by members of a minority group, or must serve one of the diverse communities in the Richmond area. Add the link to this document.

Part 2: The Objection Script. Write how you'll answer the two objections you're most likely to hear (for example, "Teenagers don't spend money at my business" or "I already sponsor another school"), using the Feel, Felt, Found method.

Part 3: The Reflection. After your role-play, write a short paragraph: How did it feel when your partner said no? What did you do to stay calm, and how did you pivot? Did you close the deal?

Do not contact any of these businesses. This is practice: no emails, calls or visits on behalf of CHHS without administration's approval.

Use these terms accurately: sponsorship, prospect, CRM, sales pipeline, objection, close.

## Background

In sports and entertainment, ticket sales often don't cover the costs of running a team or event. A big share of the money comes from sponsors: businesses that pay to put their names in front of fans. A sponsorship is a business deal, not a donation. The business pays because it gets something valuable: attention from the people at your games.

Finding prospects takes initiative. A prospect is a business that might buy. Good salespeople don't wait to be handed a list; they research who would benefit most from reaching your audience, find the right person to talk to, and keep track of every lead.

Customer relationship management (CRM) software helps salespeople track every prospect and where each one stands. Big companies use programs like Salesforce or HubSpot; a well-organized spreadsheet works the same way. The list of prospects at each stage is called the sales pipeline.

Your community is diverse, and your sponsors should reflect it. Businesses owned by women and members of minority groups, and businesses that serve the different communities of the Richmond area, are part of the CHHS fan base, and reaching out to them builds stronger community partnerships.

Objections aren't the end of the conversation. An objection is a business owner explaining what they're worried about. Stay calm, stay respectful, and answer the worry.

The Feel, Felt, Found method:

- Feel: show you understand. "I understand how you feel about spending $500 right now."
- Felt: show they're not alone. "Other local business owners felt the same way at first."
- Found: share what changed their mind. "What they found was that hundreds of families saw their name at every home game."

Only use results you can back up. Never make up a number ("they made $1,500 more") to win a sale. That's dishonest, and it destroys trust when the sponsor finds out.

Closing means asking for the commitment: "Would you like to be our banner sponsor for the season?"

## Key terms

- Sponsorship: a deal in which a business pays to connect its name to a team or event.
- Prospect: a potential customer who might buy.
- CRM (customer relationship management): a system for tracking customers, prospects and sales.
- Sales pipeline: all the prospects a salesperson is working on, grouped by stage.
- Objection: a concern a customer raises that stops them from buying.
- Close: asking for, and getting, the customer's commitment to buy.

## Example

A sample Feel, Felt, Found response, for a different objection.

- Objection: "My business is too small to spend money on advertising."
- Feel: "I completely understand. Every dollar matters for a small business."
- Felt: "Other small businesses near the school felt the same way."
- Found: "What they found is that a banner at our home games puts their name in front of the same local families who live right around the corner, all season long, for less than the cost of one newspaper ad."
