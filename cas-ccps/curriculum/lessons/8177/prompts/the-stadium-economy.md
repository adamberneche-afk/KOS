## Prompt

Assignment: The City Council Recommendation

You are a financial analyst for Chesterfield County. A minor-league team wants to build a $300 million stadium complex and is asking the county to pay half, $150 million, with a loan. Read the economic impact report below, then write a memo to the county's leaders recommending whether to approve or deny the request.

The Economic Impact Report:

- Total cost: $300 million. The team pays $150 million; the county is asked to pay $150 million.
- The county's share would be borrowed over 30 years at 6% interest: about $10.9 million a year, or about $327 million in total. (At 3% interest, it would be about $7.7 million a year, or about $230 million in total.)
- Construction costs are rising about 6% a year because of inflation. A one-year delay would add about $18 million to the $300 million price.
- The team's own study says the stadium will bring $40 million a year in new spending to the county.
- An independent economist estimates only about $12 million a year is truly new spending. The rest is money local families would have spent on other entertainment in the county anyway.
- The county collects about 5% of local spending in taxes.
- The team averages 4,500 fans a game. The proposed stadium has 12,000 seats.

Part 1: The Verdict. State clearly: Approve or Deny (you may approve with conditions).

Part 2: Spending and GDP. Explain how the stadium would affect consumer spending and the county's GDP. Which estimate of new spending do you trust more, and why?

Part 3: The Risk. Explain how the interest rate and inflation make the county's share more expensive. Use the numbers.

Part 4: The Right Size. Use the law of diminishing returns to recommend a seating capacity, and explain why.

Format it as a one-page memo (To, From, Date, Subject, then your paragraphs). Use only the numbers in the report.

Use these terms accurately: GDP, consumer spending, inflation, interest rate, municipal bond, law of diminishing returns.

## Background

Some new pro stadiums cost more than $2 billion, and teams often ask cities and counties to help pay for them. Owners say a stadium boosts the local economy. Most economists who have studied stadiums find that the public rarely gets its money back. This lesson is about weighing those two sides with numbers.

The case for a stadium:

- Construction creates jobs while it's being built.
- On game days, fans spend money at nearby restaurants, hotels and shops.
- Consumer spending is all the money people spend on goods and services. It's one of the biggest drivers of the economy.
- Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of everything produced in an area. More spending can raise local GDP.

The case against:

- Much of the spending isn't new. A family that goes to a game might skip a movie or a restaurant across town that same night. The money moved; it didn't grow.
- The county has to borrow its share by selling municipal bonds, which are loans it pays back with interest. The higher the interest rate, the more extra money taxpayers pay. Over 30 years, interest can almost double what was borrowed.
- Inflation means prices rise over time. When the cost of concrete, steel and labor goes up, the stadium costs more, and so do the tickets needed to pay for it.

The law of diminishing returns: adding more of something helps at first, then each addition helps less, until it costs more than it's worth. If a team averages 10,000 fans, a 15,000-seat stadium gives room to grow. A 40,000-seat stadium means 25,000 seats that cost money to build, clean, light and maintain, but almost never bring in a dollar.

How to compare the numbers: compare what the county pays each year (the loan payment) with what it would gain each year (taxes on truly new spending). If the payment is much bigger than the gain, the county loses money unless there's another good reason to build.

## Key terms

- Gross domestic product (GDP): the total value of all goods and services produced in an area.
- Consumer spending: the money people spend on goods and services.
- Inflation: the general rise in prices over time.
- Interest rate: the percentage a borrower pays to use borrowed money.
- Municipal bond: a loan a city, county or state takes out by selling bonds to investors, then pays back with interest.
- Law of diminishing returns: the point where adding more of something brings smaller and smaller benefits.
- Economic impact: the change in an area's economy caused by a project or event.

## Example

The same math for an imaginary town's $20 million ice rink, using different numbers from yours.

- The town would borrow $10 million. Over 20 years at 5%, it pays about $800,000 a year.
- The rink would bring about $2 million a year in truly new spending. At a 5% tax rate, the town collects about $100,000 a year.
- $800,000 paid vs. $100,000 collected: the town loses about $700,000 a year on the deal, unless the rink brings other benefits, like a place for local kids to skate, that the town decides are worth the cost.
