The Seven Eras of Marketing

Part 1: The Timeline

Trade Era (prehistoric times to the 1600s-1700s): People produced goods by hand and traded or bartered their extra goods directly with one another to get what they needed.

Production Era (mid-1800s to the 1920s): Thanks to the Industrial Revolution, businesses focused on making products quickly and cheaply in large quantities, assuming that if they built it, people would buy it.

Selling Era (1920s to the 1950s): With more products than people needed, companies used aggressive advertising and pushy salespeople to convince customers to buy whatever the company had already made.

Marketing Department Era (1950s to the 1960s): Companies created separate marketing departments that coordinated advertising, pricing, and distribution and began paying attention to what customers wanted in the short term.

Marketing Company Era (1960s to the 1990s): Marketing became the focus of the entire company, and every department worked together to satisfy customer needs and wants while still earning a profit (the marketing concept).

Relationship Marketing Era (1990s to the early 2000s): Businesses focused on building long-term relationships and loyalty with customers through loyalty programs, personalized service, and customer databases.

Collaboration Era (2000s to today): Companies collaborate with customers and partners through social media, reviews, and online communities, so customers help create, shape, and promote products.

Part 2: The Shift

The biggest difference between the Selling Era and the Marketing Company Era is who comes first. In the Selling Era, businesses made products first and then used high-pressure tactics and persuasive advertising to push them on customers, even if the customers did not really need them. In the Marketing Company Era, businesses flipped this around and started by finding out what customers actually needed and wanted, then designed products, prices, and promotions to satisfy those needs. Instead of tricking people into buying, companies tried to satisfy customers so they would come back again. For example, instead of a team just pushing season tickets with hard-sell phone calls, a team in the Marketing Company Era surveys fans and creates flexible ticket plans, family packages, and better stadium experiences that fans actually want.

Part 3: The Future

I predict the next era of marketing will be called the "Personalization and Trust Era." Artificial intelligence will allow companies to create ads, offers, and even virtual reality experiences that are customized for each individual fan, like a VR courtside seat for an NBA game or a personalized highlight reel from your favorite team. However, stronger privacy laws mean companies will have to earn customers' trust by being honest about how they use data, so the brands that win will be the ones that personalize without crossing the line.

Part 4: The Functions of Marketing

The seven functions of marketing are:
1. Channel Management (Distribution)
2. Marketing-Information Management
3. Market Planning
4. Pricing
5. Product/Service Management
6. Promotion
7. Selling

Example 1 - Pricing: Many NFL and MLB teams use dynamic pricing, which means ticket prices change based on demand. For example, tickets to a Dallas Cowboys game against a big rival cost much more than tickets to a game against a team with a losing record, and prices can rise as the game gets closer and seats sell out.

Example 2 - Promotion: Before a new Marvel movie comes out, Disney uses promotion through trailers, social media teasers, fast-food partnerships, and Super Bowl commercials. These promotions build excitement so fans buy tickets on opening weekend, which is why Marvel films often break box office records.
