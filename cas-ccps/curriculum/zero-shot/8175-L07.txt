I chose Scenario 3: The Treasurer.

In this scenario, I am the treasurer for an organization, and I am being asked to fudge the numbers in the budget to cover costs, even though everyone else in the group agrees with it. What is unethical here is that changing the numbers is dishonest. It is not just against the rules; it misleads the people who trust the budget to be accurate, like members, sponsors, or the school, and it hides the real problem instead of solving it. If the numbers are wrong once, nobody can trust the organization's records again, and as treasurer, I am the one who is responsible for that trust.

Here is how I would react:

First, I would stay calm and not accuse anyone. I would say something like, "I understand we are in a tough spot with these costs, and I want to help fix it. But I am not comfortable changing the numbers, because if anyone reviews the budget, it puts all of us at risk, and it is not honest to the people who rely on it."

Second, I would offer solutions instead of only saying no. I would suggest that we look for real ways to cover the costs, such as cutting a less important expense, running a fundraiser, asking a sponsor for help, or being upfront with our advisor about the shortfall and asking for guidance.

Third, if the group still insisted on fudging the numbers, I would document what happened and bring the issue to our advisor or supervisor privately. I would explain the situation respectfully and focus on the facts, not on blaming people.

Handling it this way protects my integrity and also protects my relationships with the group. By using "we" language and focusing on fixing the problem together, I show that I am on the team's side, not against them. Being honest might be uncomfortable for a moment, but it is much better than everyone getting in trouble later or the organization losing its reputation.
