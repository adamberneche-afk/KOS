Olymponomics: Is Hosting Worth It?

PART 1: THE RESEARCH

The Cost: Hosting the Olympics typically costs between 10 and 50 billion dollars. A study from the University of Oxford found that every Olympics since 1960 has gone over budget, with an average cost overrun of more than 150 percent. The 2014 Sochi Winter Games cost about 51 billion dollars, and the 2016 Rio Games cost around 13 billion dollars.

The Revenue: Cities make money back through ticket sales, local sponsorships, a share of the International Olympic Committee's TV broadcasting deals, and tourism. However, the IOC keeps a large share of TV money, and revenue usually covers only a fraction of the total cost.

The White Elephants: After the 2004 Athens Games, most of the venues, such as the softball stadium and the beach volleyball arena, were abandoned and left to decay, and the Games contributed to Greece's debt before its financial crisis. In Rio, the Maracana Stadium and Olympic Park fell into disrepair within months, with unpaid electricity bills and vandalism.

The Successes: The 1984 Los Angeles Olympics made a profit of about 215 million dollars. Los Angeles used existing venues like the Memorial Coliseum, relied on corporate sponsors such as McDonald's and Coca-Cola, and built very little new infrastructure.

The Economy: Greece and Brazil both entered serious economic trouble around their Games. Brazil was in a deep recession in 2016, which meant less government money for upkeep and lower tourist spending. In contrast, the United States economy was growing strongly in 1984, which helped boost sponsorships and ticket demand.

PART 2: THE ESSAY

Hosting the Olympics is usually a financial trap, not a smart investment. Research from Oxford shows that every Olympics since 1960 has gone over budget, and costs often reach tens of billions of dollars. Cities expect tourism and broadcasting money to pay them back, but the International Olympic Committee keeps much of the TV revenue, and demand for hotels and tickets disappears once the Games end.

The worst examples are Athens and Rio. Athens spent about 11 billion dollars in 2004, and many of its stadiums now sit abandoned, adding to Greece's debt. Rio spent around 13 billion dollars during a recession, and its Olympic Park was crumbling within a year. The weak economy meant there was no money to maintain these "white elephants."

Supporters point to Los Angeles in 1984, which earned a 215 million dollar profit. However, Los Angeles succeeded because it used existing venues, attracted corporate sponsors, and hosted during a growing economy. Most cities cannot copy that.

Unless a city already has the stadiums and a strong economy, hosting the Olympics costs far more than it brings back. For most cities, the glory lasts two weeks, but the debt and empty stadiums last for decades, leaving local taxpayers to pay the bill.
