Part 1: The Naming Rights Bid

Selected Brand: Chick-fil-A
Proposed Name: Chick-fil-A Field at Clover Hill High School

Proposal:
Chick-fil-A wants its name on the Clover Hill football stadium because Friday night football brings thousands of local families, students and community members together every fall, which is exactly the audience the brand serves. Putting the Chick-fil-A name on the field would keep the brand in front of fans every home game, on the scoreboard, in announcements and in local news coverage and social media posts. The partnership fits Chick-fil-A's reputation for supporting youth, education and community through programs like its Leadership Scholarships. In return, the school would receive money that could pay for new turf, uniforms or equipment, and the restaurant could offer post-game deals that bring fans straight to nearby locations. This deal builds goodwill and customer loyalty in the community for years, making it a smart investment for Chick-fil-A and a win for Clover Hill.

What each stakeholder gains: The brand gains visibility and community goodwill, the school gains funding for its athletic program, and fans gain improved facilities and game-day discounts.

Part 2: Ambush Marketing Analysis

Define It: Ambush marketing is when a company tries to connect its brand to a big event, like the Olympics or the Super Bowl, without paying to be an official sponsor. The brand advertises near the event or uses tricks to get attention so people think it is involved.

The Example: One of the most famous examples happened at the 1996 Atlanta Olympics. Reebok paid to be an official sponsor, but Nike bought billboards all around the city, set up a huge "Nike Town" store near the Olympic Village and handed out Nike banners to fans. Sprinter Michael Johnson also wore eye-catching gold Nike shoes on camera. Many people believed Nike was a sponsor even though it never paid the Olympic Committee's sponsorship fee. Another example is Beats by Dre at the 2012 London Olympics, where the company gave free headphones to athletes, who wore them on camera even though Beats was not an official sponsor. Ambush marketers get around the fees and the exclusive rights that official sponsors pay millions for, and the Olympic Committee now uses rules like Rule 40 and "clean zones" around venues to protect paying sponsors.

The Verdict: I think ambush marketing is more dishonest than genius, because it takes advantage of the attention that official sponsors paid millions of dollars to earn. It may be clever and usually legal, but it confuses customers and makes sponsorships worth less to the brands that played by the rules.

Sponsor or Endorser:
A sponsorship is when a brand pays a team, venue or event to be connected with it, such as a company paying to have its logo on a stadium or to be the "official drink" of a tournament. An endorsement is when a brand pays an individual person, usually an athlete or celebrity, to promote its product.
A brand might choose a sponsorship because it reaches a large, loyal audience of fans every game and the deal does not depend on one person's performance or behavior.
A brand might choose an endorsement because fans feel a personal connection to a star athlete, and seeing that person use the product makes it feel more trustworthy and exciting.
