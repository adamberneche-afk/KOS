Part 1: Methods of Selling Analysis

Personal Selling
Definition: Personal selling is any direct, face-to-face or one-on-one communication between a salesperson and a customer, such as talking to a customer in a store, over the phone or on a video call, with the goal of making a sale.
Benefits:
1. The salesperson can answer questions and adjust the pitch to the specific customer's needs.
2. It builds trust and long-term relationships, which leads to repeat customers.
Negatives:
1. It is expensive, because companies have to pay salaries, commissions and training costs.
2. It reaches only a small number of customers at a time.

Non-Personal Selling
Definition: Non-personal selling is reaching customers without direct one-on-one contact, using methods like advertising, websites, social media, direct mail, email and vending machines.
Benefits:
1. It reaches a large number of people at once.
2. It costs less per customer reached than having a salesperson talk to each one.
Negatives:
1. It cannot answer customer questions or handle objections in the moment.
2. It is easy for customers to ignore, skip or scroll past.

Part 2: The 6 Steps of a Sale

1. Approach the customer
2. Determine needs
3. Present the product
4. Overcome objections
5. Close the sale
6. Follow up (suggestion selling and building the relationship)

Key Concept: "Selling is helping customers solve their problems by meeting their needs and wants."

Part 3: The "No, No, Yes" Roleplay

What I sold: A season ticket package for the school's basketball games ($40 for all home games)

Opening line: "Hey! Are you planning to come to any basketball games this winter? We have a season pass that gets you into every home game for less than the price of five tickets."

Rejection 1: "No thanks, I don't really go to many games."
My response: "That makes sense. A lot of people say that, but this year we have a theme night every Friday, like a white-out and a pajama night, plus free pizza at the first game. It is a fun way to hang out with your friends on a Friday."

Rejection 2: "It still sounds like a lot of money."
My response: "I get it. Single tickets are $8, so if you come to just five games it pays for itself, and everything after that is free. You also get priority seating in the student section."

The "Yes" line: "And if you sign up today, you get a free student-section T-shirt that you can wear to the white-out game."
Customer: "Okay, yes, I'll get one."

Reflection: Being rejected twice felt awkward at first, and after the second "no" I was tempted to give up. But each rejection told me what the customer actually cared about. The first one was about interest, so I talked about the fun experience. The second was about price, so I showed the value and savings. I did change my strategy, moving from a general pitch to answering the exact objection, and adding a small bonus helped close the sale. It showed me that "no" often means "not yet" and that listening is the most important part of selling.
