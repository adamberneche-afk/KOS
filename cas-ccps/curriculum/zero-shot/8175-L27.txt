Spring Break Field Report: Marketing in the Wild

The Observation (The What)
Over Spring Break I realized marketing was everywhere, even when I was supposed to be relaxing. My family drove to Myrtle Beach, and along I-95 there were dozens of billboards for South of the Border, each with a silly joke and a mileage countdown. On my phone, I kept seeing sponsored Instagram posts from Gymshark advertising their spring collection, and on TikTok I saw creators doing "haul" videos that were clearly paid partnerships. At the movie theater, before the film, there was a trailer for a new Marvel movie and an ad for Coca-Cola. Even at the beach, a plane flew by pulling a banner for a local seafood restaurant's happy hour.

The Reverse Engineering (The How)
The campaign I want to break down is the Gymshark sponsored posts. It probably started with the marketing team setting a goal, like increasing spring sales by 15 percent. They would research their target market, which is people roughly 16 to 30 who work out and follow fitness creators. The chief marketing officer or marketing director would likely have to approve the budget, deciding how much to spend on influencer deals and paid ads. Then a creative team, including graphic designers, photographers and copywriters, would design the visuals and write short captions. They would hire models or fitness influencers for photo shoots, then the social media team would schedule the posts and use Instagram's ad tools to target users based on age, location and interests like "fitness" and "gym."

Why that platform: Gymshark chose Instagram and TikTok because that is where people my age scroll every day and follow fitness creators. Ads there look like normal posts, so they feel less like advertising.

Keeping You
After I clicked on the ad, Gymshark tried hard to keep me. A pop-up offered 10 percent off my first order if I signed up for their email list, and I did. Since then I have received emails about new drops and a "you left something in your cart" reminder. They also pushed me to download the Gymshark app, which has a training feature, to keep me coming back even when I am not shopping.

Was It Worth It
Gymshark could tell whether the campaign earned back its cost by tracking results. Each ad link and discount code can be traced, so they can see how many people clicked, signed up and bought. They would compare the total sales from those clicks and codes to the amount spent on ads and influencers. If they spent $50,000 and made $200,000 in sales, the return on investment would be strong. They could also measure new followers, email sign-ups and repeat purchases to see the long-term value.

Overall, this assignment made me realize that almost every ad I saw was carefully planned, tested and measured, not random.
