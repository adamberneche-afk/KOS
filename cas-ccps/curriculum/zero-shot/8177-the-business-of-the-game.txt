The Corporate Scouting Report: The Dallas Cowboys

Part 1: The Company
The Dallas Cowboys are a National Football League team based in Arlington, Texas. According to Forbes' 2025 NFL team valuations, the Cowboys are worth about $13 billion, making them the most valuable sports franchise in the world for the tenth year in a row. That valuation is what the team would be estimated to sell for, not the cash it has in the bank.

Part 2: The Owner
The Cowboys have been owned by Jerry Jones and his family since 1989, when he bought the team for about $150 million. Even though people say the team is "owned by one family," it is not a sole proprietorship. The Joneses control the team through a privately held company, so the business is legally a corporation. I know this because the NFL does not allow a single person to personally own a team as a sole proprietorship, and the team's business filings and Forbes describe it as a company controlled by the Jones family. Because it is a corporation, the family is protected from losing more than what they invested. Jerry Jones also serves as president and general manager, so the person who takes the financial risk is also the person who makes the big decisions, from hiring coaches to building AT&T Stadium.

Part 3: The Money
1. Media rights: The NFL sells the rights to broadcast games to networks like CBS, FOX, NBC, ESPN and Amazon, and splits that money evenly among all 32 teams, giving each team hundreds of millions of dollars a year.
2. Ticket sales and premium seating: AT&T Stadium holds about 80,000 fans, and the Cowboys earn a lot of revenue from luxury suites, club seats and season tickets, which cost far more than regular seats.
3. Sponsorships and stadium deals: Companies like AT&T pay millions of dollars a year to put their name on the stadium and their logos around the field, and the Cowboys also earn money from concerts and other events held at the stadium.

Merchandise is also huge for "America's Team," but the top three above bring in the most revenue. Player salaries are the biggest expense, and the team's profit is what's left after salaries, staff, travel and stadium costs are paid.

Part 4: The Community
AT&T Stadium was built in Arlington in 2009 for about $1.2 billion, and the city of Arlington paid about $325 million of that through higher sales, hotel and car rental taxes approved by voters. The good side is that the stadium brings in visitors, creates game-day jobs and hosts big events like the College Football Playoff and concerts. The bad side is that local taxpayers helped pay for a stadium owned by one of the richest teams in the world, and some economists argue the city won't fully earn that money back.

Part 5: The Competition
The Cowboys' direct competition is other sports teams in the Dallas area, like the Texas Rangers or Dallas Mavericks. Their biggest indirect competitor on a Friday night is a concert or a night out in Dallas, such as going to a show at the American Airlines Center or the movies. A family of four could easily spend $600 or more on a Cowboys game, while a movie and dinner costs a fraction of that. Fans have limited money and time, so anything else fun they can do that night competes for the same dollars.

Comparison with the Green Bay Packers
The Packers are a nonprofit owned by fans, so they can never be moved and no single owner can make decisions for their own profit. The Cowboys' for-profit ownership is the opposite: Jerry Jones can make decisions quickly and invest heavily, like building a huge stadium, but the team's future depends on one family's choices.
