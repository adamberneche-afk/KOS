The Front Lines

Part 1: The Sales Script (Scenario A: the 12-seat suite, $15,000 for the season)

Step 1: Approach
"Good afternoon, thank you so much for meeting with me today. I'm an account executive with the team, and I've driven past your dealership for years. It's one of the busiest on the boulevard. I'd love to learn a little about your business and see if our suite program could be a good fit for you."

Step 2: Determine needs (open-ended questions)
- "Who are the most important people you need to build relationships with: your customers, your employees or business partners?"
- "How do you usually thank your top customers or reward your sales team right now?"
- "What does a successful marketing investment look like for your dealership?"
Owner's likely answer: they want to thank loyal customers and motivate their salespeople, but they aren't sure a suite is worth the cost.

Step 3: Present the product (features into benefits)
- Feature: the suite has 12 seats for every home game. Benefit: "You can invite a different group of your best customers and their families every week, so dozens of loyal buyers get a night they'll remember and associate with your dealership."
- Feature: food and drinks can be catered right to the suite. Benefit: "You don't have to plan anything. Your guests get a great meal, and you get two and a half hours of relaxed time with them, which is often where the next sale or referral happens."
- Feature: your dealership name appears on the suite door and is announced on the PA once a season. Benefit: "Thousands of local fans see your name every game, the same people who will be buying their next car in this area."

Step 4: Overcome objections
Objection: "$15,000 seems like a lot, and I'm not sure we'd use it."
"I completely understand, it's a real investment. Let's look at it a different way. With about 70 home games, that's roughly $214 a game for 12 seats, less than $18 a person. If even one customer you host buys a car or sends you a referral, the suite has likely paid for itself. And if you can't use a game, you could give those seats to your sales team as a reward or to a local charity, so no night goes to waste."

Step 5: Close
"Based on what you've told me about rewarding your top customers and your sales team, I think this suite fits your goals. Would you like to reserve it for the season so we can lock in your opening-night date?"

Step 6: Suggestion selling
"Great decision. Many of our suite partners also add our VIP parking package for $1,200, which gives your guests reserved spots right next to the gate. It makes the whole experience feel first class from the moment they arrive. Would you like me to add that?"

Part 2: The Service Script (Scenario B: the seat behind the pillar, using T.H.A.N.K.S.)

Thank: "Thank you for coming to the box office and telling me about this. I'm glad you came to us so we can make it right."

Hear them out: (I let the fan explain everything without interrupting, nodding and keeping my voice calm.) "I hear you. You paid for a great seat and you can't see the field."

Apologize: "I'm truly sorry. You paid $150 to enjoy the game, and sitting behind a pillar is not the experience you deserve."

Need more information: "Can I see your ticket? Which section and seat are you in? Are you here with anyone else who needs to move with you? Would you prefer to stay for tonight's game if we can find you a better seat?"

Know a solution: I know I'm allowed to offer a seat change, a free future ticket or a refund.

Solve the problem: "Here's what I can do. I'm moving you to an open seat in Section 104, about the same price level, with a clear view of home plate. I'm also giving you a voucher for a free ticket to a future game of your choice, plus a $20 concession credit for tonight. Does that work for you?" Then I check back with them during the game to make sure they're satisfied.

Why this is fair to both: the fan gets what they paid for, a great view tonight, plus a little extra to make up for the frustration. The team keeps the $150 sale, fills an empty seat and turns an angry fan into a loyal one. If no equal seat were available, I would offer the full refund. Good service recovery leads to customer retention: a fan who leaves happier than before is more likely to come back and tell friends how well they were treated.

Part 3: Reflect

Scenario A, what happened: I opened with my approach line and asked both open-ended questions. My partner said the dealership mainly wanted to reward its sales team. I used the "less than $18 a person" line when my partner said $15,000 was too much. My partner then asked whether they could buy half a season. I offered a 35-game half-season suite for $8,000, and my partner agreed to it.

Scenario B, what happened: My partner came in angry and talked over me at first. I used my Thank and Apologize lines and stayed quiet while they vented. When I offered the new seat, the voucher and the concession credit, my partner said they still wanted their money back. I explained the refund option was available but that the new seat was in a better spot than their original one, and they decided to accept the seat change and the voucher.

Hardest step: overcoming objections in the sales scenario. It was hard not to sound pushy when my partner kept saying the suite was too expensive.

What surprised me: my partner asked for a half season, which I hadn't planned for.

How I adjusted: instead of repeating the same value argument, I asked another open-ended question about how many games they could really use, then offered a smaller package that still met their needs. I learned that listening and being flexible works better than sticking exactly to a script.
