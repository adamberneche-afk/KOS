The International Expansion Proposal: The Golden State Warriors in Paris

Part 1: The Destination
Brand: The Golden State Warriors (NBA)
City: Paris, France

Why there's a market: France is one of the strongest basketball countries outside the U.S. The French national team won silver at the 2024 Paris Olympics, and French players like Victor Wembanyama have become huge NBA stars, so French fans watch the NBA more than ever. The NBA already held regular-season games in Paris starting in 2020, and those games sold out the Accor Arena (about 15,000 to 16,000 seats) quickly. Paris has more than 12 million people in its metro area, and the Warriors are one of the most popular NBA teams in Europe because of Stephen Curry. Our event would be a regular-season game at Accor Arena plus a free fan festival and youth clinic.

Part 2: The Money
Local currency: the euro (€)
General admission ticket price: €75
Today's exchange rate: 1 euro = 1.16 U.S. dollars (source: Google Finance)
Math: €75 × 1.16 = $87.00
So a €75 ticket is worth about $87.00.

Merchandise example: Warriors jersey at €110 × 1.16 = $127.60

Note on the dollar: If the dollar gets stronger, each euro we earn turns into fewer dollars back home, so our revenue in dollars would go down. If the dollar gets weaker, the same ticket sales turn into more dollars.

Part 3: The Culture
Cultural adaptation: We would partner with a popular French player and a French rapper to host the fan festival and perform at halftime, and translate our "Strength in Numbers" slogan into French as "La Force du Collectif," which keeps the meaning of teamwork instead of a word-for-word translation that might sound awkward. At concessions, we would add French favorites like jambon-beurre sandwiches, crêpes and local cheeses along with classic American hot dogs, so the event feels like a mix of both cultures. We would also schedule the game at 8:00 PM Paris time, when French fans normally go out for evening events; that's 2:00 PM in Richmond, so U.S. fans could still watch live. French fans would respond well because the event would feel made for them, not just an American game dropped into their city.

Part 4: The Challenge
Challenge: Jet lag and the time zone change. Paris is 6 hours ahead of Richmond and 9 hours ahead of San Francisco, so the players' bodies would be badly out of sync and could play poorly or risk injury.
How we'd handle it: The team would fly to Paris five days before the game on an overnight charter flight so players can sleep on the plane, then follow a sleep plan from the team's sports scientists, with light exposure and practice times set to Paris time. We'd schedule a light practice and community clinic early in the trip and give the team several days off after returning home.

Another challenge: Shipping equipment through customs. All team equipment, merchandise and broadcast gear would need customs paperwork and could be delayed. We'd use an ATA Carnet, a customs document for temporary imports, and ship everything three weeks early through an experienced logistics company.

Cultural and political factors: France has a value-added tax (VAT) of 20% on most goods, so merchandise sales would bring in less profit than in the U.S. unless prices are set to account for it. France also has strict rules on alcohol advertising at sports events (the Loi Évin), so beer sponsors that are common at U.S. games couldn't be promoted, which could lower sponsorship revenue.

Global trade connection: The event itself is an export, since we're selling a U.S. entertainment product overseas, and the broadcast rights for French TV are another export. Jerseys made in Southeast Asia and sold in Paris show how global trade works across several countries at once.
