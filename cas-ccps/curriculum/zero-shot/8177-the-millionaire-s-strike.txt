The Millionaire's Strike

Part 1: Your Side
I am on the Players Association.

Revenue sharing: We want a 50/50 split of TV money. It's fair because fans tune in to watch the players, not the owners. Without the players' skills, there would be no product for networks to pay for.

Season length: We refuse to add 2 more games. It's fair because every extra game increases the risk of serious injuries like concussions and torn ligaments, and players' careers are already short. More games mean more money for owners but more physical risk for players.

Minimum salary: We want rookies to earn at least $1,000,000. It's fair because the average career is only a few years long, and many rookies need to support themselves and their families with what they earn before injuries end their careers. League revenue keeps growing, so the minimum should grow too.

Overseas play: We want the right to play anywhere in the off-season. It's fair because players are workers, and workers should be able to earn money and keep their skills sharp in the off-season, especially players with lower salaries.

Part 2: The Deal
Negotiation notes (Revenue sharing):
1. Owners: 55% owners / 45% players.
2. Players: 50/50.
3. Owners: 54/46 if players agree to 2 extra games.
4. Players: 51/49 owners, no extra games.
5. Owners: 52/48, with 1 extra game.
6. Players: 52/48, no extra games, but we accept the owners' minimum salary of $850,000.
7. Owners: Accept 52/48 with no extra games if the overseas rule includes injury insurance.
8. Players: Agree.

Final agreement:
- Revenue sharing: 52% owners / 48% players. Players gave up the full 50/50; owners gave up 3 percentage points.
- Season length: No extra games. Owners gave up the extra games in exchange for a better revenue split.
- Minimum salary: $850,000 for rookies, rising $25,000 each year of the CBA. Players gave up the $1,000,000 minimum; owners raised their offer by $100,000.
- Overseas play: Players may play overseas in the off-season if they buy injury insurance approved by the team. Owners gave up the full ban; players accepted the insurance requirement.
This collective bargaining agreement settles the season and gives both sides something they wanted: owners get a bigger share of TV money and protection against injuries overseas, and players get a higher minimum salary and no extra games.

Part 3: The Collateral Damage Report
1. Concession workers at The Diamond (Flying Squirrels): These are hourly workers who only get paid when there's a game. With about 70 home games a season, a canceled season wipes out their whole game-day income, and many depend on it to pay for rent, a car or school.
2. Restaurants and bars near The Diamond and on the Boulevard: Places in Scott's Addition and along the Boulevard get a rush of fans before and after games. Without games, they lose those customers, sell less food, and may cut servers' and bartenders' hours, meaning less in tips and wages.
3. Parking attendants and rideshare drivers: Attendants who work game-day lots are paid by the event, and Uber and Lyft drivers make extra money taking fans to and from games. A canceled season means those shifts and rides disappear.

When many workers lose their paychecks at once, they spend less at grocery stores, shops and other restaurants. Those businesses then earn less and may cut workers too, so the effect spreads and unemployment rises across the city.

Part 4: Who Has the Leverage?
Owners can't just fire striking players and hire replacements because of specialization. Pro athletes have rare skills that almost no one else has, and if owners replaced them with amateurs, the quality of play would drop sharply and fans would stop buying tickets and watching on TV. The owners' TV deals and ticket sales depend on having the best players. Players who can play overseas have even more leverage because they have another way to earn money. During the 2011 NBA lockout, some players signed with teams in Europe and Asia. Owners can't move their stadiums or their TV deals overseas, so they lose money every day there are no games, while those players keep earning.

Part 5: The Law
The National Labor Relations Act (1935) gives players the right to form a union and bargain collectively, which means they negotiate as a group instead of one at a time, and owners can't refuse to negotiate with the union. Owners would rather have the draft and salary cap written into a CBA because of antitrust law. Under the Sherman Antitrust Act, if all the owners simply agreed among themselves to limit where players can go or how much they can be paid, it would look like competitors teaming up to hold down wages, and players could sue. But rules agreed to with the players' union in a CBA are protected from antitrust lawsuits. That's why owners actually need a CBA, and why in 2011 the NFL and NBA players' unions disbanded so players could sue under antitrust law and pressure owners to make a deal.

Key terms: union, collective bargaining agreement, strike, lockout, leverage, specialization, unemployment, labor law, antitrust law.
