MEMORANDUM

To: Chesterfield County Board of Supervisors
From: Financial Analyst, Chesterfield County Department of Budget and Management
Date: November 20, 2026
Subject: Recommendation on the proposed $300 million stadium complex

Part 1: The Verdict
I recommend that the county DENY the request to borrow $150 million for the proposed stadium complex as written. The county would pay far more each year on its loan than it would collect in new tax revenue. I would be willing to reconsider a smaller, roughly 6,000-seat stadium with a lower county share, a fixed price, and a team guarantee to stay in Chesterfield for the life of the loan.

Part 2: Spending and GDP
Supporters argue that the stadium would increase consumer spending at restaurants, hotels and shops on game days, which would raise the county's GDP. The team's study claims $40 million a year in new spending, but I trust the independent economist's estimate of $12 million more. The team has a financial interest in making the project look as good as possible, while the independent economist does not. The independent estimate also accounts for the fact that most of the spending is not new: families who attend games would otherwise spend that money on movies, bowling or restaurants elsewhere in the county. That money moves; it doesn't grow, so it adds little to GDP. At a 5% tax rate, $12 million in new spending brings in only about $600,000 a year in taxes. Even using the team's $40 million estimate, the county would collect just $2 million a year.

Part 3: The Risk
The county would pay for its share by issuing municipal bonds. At a 6% interest rate over 30 years, the county would pay about $10.9 million a year, or about $327 million in total, more than double the $150 million borrowed. Even at 3% interest, the payments would be about $7.7 million a year, or $230 million total. Comparing the yearly payment of $10.9 million with the $600,000 in yearly taxes, the county would lose about $10.3 million every year. Even with the team's optimistic estimate ($2 million in taxes), the loss would still be about $8.9 million a year. Inflation adds more risk: construction costs are rising about 6% a year, so a one-year delay would add about $18 million to the $300 million price, raising the amount that might need to be borrowed.

Part 4: The Right Size
The law of diminishing returns shows that the proposed 12,000-seat stadium is too large. The team averages 4,500 fans a game, so 7,500 seats would sit empty most nights while still costing money to build, clean, light and maintain. Adding seats helps at first by giving room for big games and growth, but beyond a certain point each additional seat brings in almost nothing. I recommend a capacity of about 6,000 to 6,500 seats, which gives the team room to grow by about a third while lowering construction costs and the amount the county would need to borrow.

Conclusion
The stadium might bring community benefits, such as a place for families to gather, high school championships and summer concerts. Those benefits are real, but they do not close a gap of about $10 million a year. Taxpayers across the county would pay for the loan, while most of the profits would go to the team's owners. For these reasons, I recommend that the Board deny the request as proposed.
