# Assumptions Deep Dive — "The Economics of Depth"

*A structural audit of the white paper's load-bearing claims. For each assumption: why it's structural (the argument collapses or weakens significantly if it's false), what Adam's own Drive contains that bears on it, what external literature says, and a steel-manned case for both sides. This document does not resolve the assumptions — it's meant to inform Part I's Open Question A1 and A2, and to give a colleague/administrator audience a fair sense of what's solid ground and what's live debate.*

---

## How "structural" was determined

Five assumptions were selected from roughly a dozen claims in the paper because each one, if false, doesn't just weaken a section — it breaks the *chain* the argument depends on. A claim that's merely colorful (Dunbar's numbers, the Apollo Syndrome citation) was left out even where interesting, because the paper's thesis survives without it. These five don't.

---

## Assumption 1: The labor/wage divergence is real, severe, and durable

**Why structural:** This is the paper's foundation. If AI displacement doesn't meaningfully outpace historical patterns of labor reallocation, there is no "post-labor economy" to build a framework around — Parts I through IV all inherit this premise without re-testing it.

**Internal Drive evidence — direct contradiction.** A document in Adam's own Drive, "What jobs will make the most money in 20 years?" (a saved Gemini conversation, dated August 2025 — eleven months *before* "Labor and wage divergence" was written), works from a materially different assumption. It identifies AI/Data Science, biotech, and renewable energy as durable high-earning fields and concludes that "the highest-paying jobs are at the intersection of technology, humanity, and communication" — a conventional, optimistic labor-market framing with no mention of proxy payers, unscalability premiums, or economic depth. This is either evidence of evolved thinking over eleven months, or two unreconciled models coexisting in the same Drive. Worth noting directly: **this is not a hypothetical tension — it's the same author, addressing the same underlying question, reaching structurally different conclusions eleven months apart.**

**External evidence — genuinely, actively contested.** This is not settled science in either direction:
- *Supporting the paper's premise:* By mid-2026, over 200 economists — including Nobel laureates Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, previously *skeptics* of large-scale AI displacement — signed a joint statement warning AI could trigger white-collar disruption at a scale prior technology waves did not. The distinguishing argument: past automation displaced "mechanical muscle" but still required human operators and supervisors; AI displaces cognitive work directly, without the same compensating demand for human cognitive labor.
- *Contradicting the paper's premise:* The dominant academic framework (Acemoglu-Restrepo, MIT/Econometrica) treats automation as task-level, not job-level — a job is a bundle of tasks, and automating one task (the "displacement effect") is partly offset by a "productivity effect" that increases demand for the tasks that remain human. Historical precedent (industrialization, computerization of clerical work) triggered the same fears and, in the multi-decade view, expanded total employment rather than shrinking it.

**Steel-man for (the divergence is real and different this time):** Every prior automation wave left a "coordination shell" of human judgment around the automated task — a lawyer loses the drafting subtask but the lawyering function persists. *Agentic* AI is different in kind, not degree: it doesn't just automate a subtask, it can coordinate the whole bundle of tasks a job consists of, including the judgment and client-communication layer previously assumed automation-proof. If that's true, the historical "it always works out" pattern is a poor guide to this specific transition.

**Steel-man against (this is the same fear, differently dressed):** Economists have been "directionally right but temporally wrong" about automation-driven job loss for decades — industrial robots did displace manufacturing workers, but nothing close to the initial forecasts, on nothing close to the initial timeline. The 200-economist letter is a statement of concern, not a predictive model with falsifiable claims; a short, unambiguous public letter is easier to get signatures on than a specific, testable forecast, which is itself a reason for caution about over-reading it as consensus. Net job-loss projections that do exist (World Economic Forum: -14 million net over five years, about 2% of employment) are real but far short of "post-labor economy" territory.

**Assessment:** Genuinely unresolved, and reasonable experts occupy both positions right now. The paper is entitled to build on this premise, but should not present it as settled — and given Adam's own Drive contains a document reaching the opposite practical conclusion less than a year earlier, this is the single assumption most worth being explicit about treating as a bet, not a finding.

## Assumption 2: AI's comparative disadvantage is unscalable human depth/connection

**Why structural:** This is the paper's core value-differentiation claim — the entire "Economy of Depth" only functions if there's something durable AI structurally cannot do at scale. If AI can produce something indistinguishable from depth (or if humans stop caring about the difference), the whole framework's value proposition collapses into "AI is currently a bit worse at empathy," which is a much weaker and more time-limited claim.

**Internal Drive evidence — no direct match found.** Nothing in the audited Drive directly tests or discusses this specific claim (as distinct from Assumption 1). This is worth naming as a gap rather than filling it with inference.

**External context (not independently verified via search for this pass — flagged as lower-confidence than Assumptions 1, 3, 4, and 5 below):** This question sits inside a live and unresolved debate in AI/philosophy-of-mind discourse about whether AI companionship and AI-mediated relationships can produce psychologically real belonging for the human on the other end, independent of whether the AI itself "feels" anything. If large numbers of people report genuine attachment to AI companions regardless of authenticity in any deeper sense, the paper's premise that *depth specifically requires a human on the other end* is empirically shaky, not just philosophically debatable.

**Steel-man for (depth requires a human, structurally):** The paper's own logic is coherent on its own terms — connection that is *known* to be manufactured for scale (a company "producing" warmth for millions of customers) is detectable and devalues itself the moment it's recognized as a product. Even a perfectly emotionally-convincing AI companion is, in principle, infinitely reproducible — and the paper's thesis is specifically that scarcity-through-unscalability is what creates value, not the emotional experience in isolation.

**Steel-man against (this may already be empirically false, not just philosophically contested):** If a user's subjective experience of connection doesn't distinguish between AI-mediated and human-mediated depth — and early evidence on AI-companion attachment suggests many users' subjective experience genuinely doesn't — then the "humans are structurally better at depth" claim may be a claim about *current* AI capability, not a durable economic law. That would mean the whole framework has an unstated expiration date the paper doesn't acknowledge.

**Assessment:** The least externally verified of the five assumptions in this document — worth flagging to a reader as an open research question rather than treating it as resolved either way, and worth a follow-up pass specifically on this claim before the paper leans on it further.

## Assumption 3: AI-driven deflation will collide with debt-based finance, resolved via currency debasement

**Why structural:** This is the mechanism for Part III's claim that the transition is *financially violent*, not smooth — and it's the bridge connecting classroom-scale reasoning to systemic-scale consequences. Without it, Part III's argument (and its rhetorically satisfying "trust resolves both problems" convergence, flagged separately as Open Question A2) has nothing to stand on.

**Internal Drive evidence:** None found relevant to this specific claim.

**External evidence — real position, but ideologically concentrated.** The theoretical foundation is legitimate and old: Irving Fisher's debt-deflation theory (1933), still cited seriously in macroeconomics, holds that falling prices increase the real burden of fixed debts, triggering default cascades. Contemporary voices making the specific "AI deflation collides with debt-saturated economy → debasement" argument are real and numerous — but the sources found making this exact case (ZeroHedge, International Man, hard-asset/gold-oriented financial commentary, the "debasement trade" now popular enough that Charles Schwab publishes investor explainers on it) skew toward a specific ideological tradition (hard-money/Austrian-adjacent, skeptical of fiat currency and central banking) rather than mainstream macroeconomic consensus. That doesn't make the argument wrong, but it means the paper is building on one school of thought's interpretation of a real, contested theoretical mechanism, not a neutral synthesis.

**Steel-man for:** The structural math is not controversial — a debt-saturated system (the paper's "$133 trillion global bond market" is a real, verifiable figure) genuinely cannot absorb a rapid deflationary shock without triggering the same default-cascade dynamics Fisher described in 1933, and central banks have a well-documented, repeated historical pattern of choosing debasement over deflation when forced to pick (this is precisely what quantitative easing after 2008 already was).

**Steel-man against:** "Technology causes deflation, deflation is scary for debtors, therefore debasement is coming" has been argued continuously by the same intellectual tradition for over a decade, through multiple technology cycles that didn't produce the predicted crisis on the predicted timeline — this is structurally the same "directionally right, temporally wrong" pattern flagged in Assumption 1's steel-man against. Mainstream macro commentary (Schwab's own framing) treats debasement as one plausible scenario among several, explicitly noting that "the impacts of modern currency debasement may not surface in the way they have historically" given how different today's globally-interlinked, central-bank-dominated system is from 16th-century England or the classical gold-standard defaults Fisher studied.

**Assessment:** A real, legitimate economic mechanism, but the paper's specific packaging of it draws disproportionately from one ideological tradition's interpretation. Worth explicitly attributing that lineage in the paper rather than presenting it as neutral macro analysis — a colleague or administrator with any finance background is likely to recognize the framing and will trust the paper more, not less, if that's acknowledged directly.

## Assumption 4: Maslow's hierarchy "mutates" — the safety floor is structurally guaranteed by abundance, without flipping the order

**Why structural:** This is the load-bearing premise of Part II. If the safety floor isn't actually guaranteed — if material insecurity remains real for a meaningful share of students regardless of broader technological abundance — then the psychological reframing (belonging and esteem as where value concentrates) rests on a foundation that doesn't hold for the students who need it most.

**Internal Drive evidence — corrected, and actually supportive, not contradictory.** Adam's own "CE-PRD: Sublimation Distribution Chain & Macro-Economy" simulation design includes an "Individual Mastery Floor (70%)... that no peer can lower" — a guaranteed baseline insulated from competitive market performance. This is structurally the same move as the paper's "safety floor is guaranteed" claim, applied at the classroom-grading level rather than the societal-abundance level — a genuine point of consistency. **On the apparent scale-vs-depth tension flagged in an earlier draft of this document: Adam clarified that this was a misreading.** The simulation's economies-of-scale mechanics (currency, negotiated B2B pricing, volume targets, quality gates) are not competing with the paper's depth-economy thesis — they're the *substrate* it's built on. The design intent is that economies of depth develop *within* the classroom specifically because the supply chain is fully visible end-to-end: every student can see exactly which node their output depends on and which node depends on them. That visibility is what turns a set of scale-based transactions into a legible trust network — which is a direct, deliberate operationalization of Part I's "Bridge Economy" and "structural holes" concepts (a trusted node spanning otherwise-disconnected parts of a network), not a departure from them. The scale mechanics create the conditions; the visible network is where the depth economy actually forms.

**External evidence — the underlying model is weak, not strong.** Maslow's hierarchy has never had solid empirical grounding, a fact acknowledged even by sympathetic sources. The most-cited empirical reckoning (Wahba and Bridwell, 1976, reviewing ten factor-analytic studies plus multiple longitudinal investigations) found only partial support for a need hierarchy at all, with longitudinal tests of Maslow's core gratification-then-progression mechanism showing "essentially no support." Real-world counterexamples are common and well-documented — people pursue belonging or purpose while physical needs remain unmet (the paper's own "freedom fighter" or "struggling artist" type cases appear repeatedly in the critical literature). Contemporary alternatives (Self-Determination Theory, ERG theory) are generally considered more empirically defensible.

**Steel-man for (the mutation framing is a legitimate move):** The paper doesn't actually claim the *original, unmodified* Maslow hierarchy is empirically solid — it explicitly reframes and mutates it, arguing the *definition* of safety shifts to social-threat-absence once abundance handles the physiological floor. This sidesteps some of the "strict sequential ordering" criticism, since the paper isn't claiming people wait for safety before pursuing belonging — it's claiming the two are woven together via redefinition. Used descriptively (a useful lens for organizing classroom design decisions) rather than prescriptively (a scientific law), the framework can be valuable independent of Maslow's own empirical weaknesses — this is explicitly how several modern positive-psychology sources suggest using it.

**Steel-man against:** Borrowing a famous but empirically weak model and "mutating" it doesn't fix the underlying evidentiary problem — it adds a new, untested claim (that abundance reliably guarantees the physiological/safety floor for all students) on top of an already-shaky one. This critique stands independent of the scale-vs-depth question above, which Adam's clarification resolves — but the weakness of Maslow's own empirical base doesn't go away just because the simulation's design intent is coherent with the paper.

**Assessment:** The weakest-sourced assumption of the five on its own terms (the underlying psychological model itself), but the paper's mutation is a defensible descriptive move if labeled as such rather than as established science. The simulation-design tension flagged in an earlier pass of this document was a misreading, corrected above — the actual open question here is narrower than it first appeared: not "does Adam's practice contradict his theory," but "is Maslow's hierarchy, even mutated, a sound enough psychological foundation to build Part II on."

## Assumption 5: AI-imposed "friction" (withholding answers) preserves student engagement rather than causing disengagement

**Why structural:** This is Part IV's central operational bet — the entire KOS system is built on the premise that deliberately frustrating students (via Agency Safeguards, Wait-Time Protocol) produces better outcomes than a more accommodating AI would. If friction just causes disengagement, the whole operationalization layer backfires.

**Internal Drive evidence:** None found directly relevant.

**External evidence — this is the best-supported assumption in the entire paper.** Unlike the economic and psychological claims above, this one rests on a genuinely mature, well-cited research tradition:
- Bjork and Bjork's "desirable difficulties" (a research program dating to 1994, not a novel idea) has repeated empirical support: conditions that slow initial performance (retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, generating an answer before seeing it) reliably improve long-term retention and transfer.
- Directly on point for AI specifically: a randomized controlled trial (Bastani et al., 2025) of nearly a thousand high-school math students found that *unguarded* AI access left students about 17% worse on an unaided exam than students with no AI access at all — while the *same underlying model*, redesigned to withhold direct answers and offer guided hints instead, erased that harm entirely and, in a related high-school study, nearly doubled short-term learning gains compared to unrestricted access.
- This is precisely the design KOS's Agency Safeguards propose — the empirical case for "guarded, answer-withholding AI beats both unguarded AI and no AI" is unusually direct and well-matched to the actual proposed mechanism.

**Steel-man for:** This isn't a contested claim requiring a steel-man on faith — it's converging evidence across cognitive psychology (Bjork), a large field RCT (Bastani), and multiple independent 2026 literature reviews, all pointing the same direction. The "desirable difficulty" framing and KOS's Friction Injection are, in substance, the same mechanism with different names.

**Steel-man against:** The research also identifies a real calibration risk the paper doesn't fully address: difficulty has to land in a specific effective range (one source cites roughly 85% success rate as the target zone) — *excessive* struggle stops being desirable and starts producing the floundering, guessing-randomly failure mode, or the "reverse nihilism"-adjacent disengagement the paper itself worries about elsewhere. There's also a documented metacognitive trap: students in productively-difficult active-learning environments consistently *feel* like they're learning less even when they're measurably learning more (Deslauriers et al.) — meaning KOS may need to actively manage student *perception* of the friction, not just the friction itself, or risk students (and possibly parents or administrators observing a lesson) concluding the system isn't working even when it is.

**Assessment:** Strongest ground in the whole paper. The main risk isn't whether the underlying principle is sound — it clearly is — but whether KOS's specific calibration and communication about *why* the friction exists will actually land in the effective range rather than the counterproductive one. This is an implementation-quality question, not a foundational-assumption question, which is a meaningfully different (and more tractable) kind of risk than Assumptions 1-4 carry.
